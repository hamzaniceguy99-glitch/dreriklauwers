The document nobody reads

The admission declaration decides a large part of a hospital bill, and it is signed at the worst possible moment to read anything.

How we work

Why room choice is a financial decision

Belgian hospital billing contains a rule that is unusually clear and unusually little known. Fee supplements may not be charged in a two-bed room or in a common room. That prohibition does not depend on the treating physician's own position in relation to the national agreement concluded between physicians and the sickness funds; it applies whatever that position is. In a single room, by contrast, supplements may be charged, within the limits and the maximum percentages applied by the hospital. The practical consequence is that a decision presented at admission as a matter of comfort and privacy is simultaneously a decision about money, and the two halves are rarely presented together with equal clarity.

Where the information does appear is the admission declaration, a document the patient receives and signs before admission setting out the financial conditions of the stay, including what each type of room means. It is a genuinely important piece of paper, and it is handed over at the precise moment a person is least equipped to study it: on arrival, often anxious, usually in a queue, among a stack of other forms. The advice that follows is simple and worth repeating because so few people act on it. Read it rather than sign it, and where an admission is planned rather than urgent, ask for it in advance so it can be read somewhere other than a reception desk.

The second point on this page concerns titles. A medical specialist title in Belgium is a recognition granted by the competent federal authority following approved training, not a description a practitioner may adopt because it matches the work they do, and the use of a specialist title without that recognition is prohibited. For that reason the recognition held is stated on the legal page rather than deployed as an adjective here, and nothing beyond what is stated there is claimed. The remaining obligations are the ones that attach to any consultation: consent that follows information about purpose, nature, urgency, risks, alternatives and financial consequences; a carefully kept record with rights of access and copy; privacy; recourse to a mediation service; and the entitlement to a second opinion, which this practice treats as ordinary rather than as a challenge.
